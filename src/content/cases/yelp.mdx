In early March 2026, Yelp's Chief Strategy Officer had two documents on her desk. One was a term sheet from a second major AI platform wanting to license Yelp's review data, on the heels of a deal already signed with OpenAI. The other was the February analytics brief: advertising revenue from restaurants, retail, and other consumer categories had fallen for a fourth straight quarter, down 12% year-over-year in the last one alone. Read together, the two documents describe the same disintermediation from opposite sides — Yelp's content is more in demand than it's ever been, and fewer people are visiting Yelp to get it.

The categories most exposed to this shift are contracting the fastest: advertising from restaurants and retail, the categories most likely to get answered directly by an AI summary, fell 6% to $444M, with the fourth quarter alone down 12%. Meanwhile Services advertising (plumbing, home repair, auto, categories where an AI answer still has to hand off to booking an actual appointment) grew 8% to a record $948M. One complicating detail worth holding onto: total search demand rose 26% globally over the same period. The value chain didn't shrink. It restructured. Yelp's share of it moved to wherever the click used to land.

This is [Attract](/workflows/attract)'s citation-signal problem playing out at the level of an entire company's strategy, not a single page's SEO. The same four signals — provenance, structure, specificity, recency — that decide whether any individual page gets cited decide, in aggregate, whether Yelp's reviews get cited at all; the strategic question is what Yelp does once being cited stops reliably sending anyone to Yelp.

<Section index="03" kicker="What went right or wrong" title="The tradeoffs, weighed honestly">

Yelp's own assets cut in different directions depending on which option they support. The 330M+ review corpus, refreshed by 22 million new reviews a year, is exactly the kind of current, deep, long-tail content an answer engine has trouble replacing with a general web scrape. That's a genuine argument for the Source Layer Pivot, where being the best underlying data source is the whole strategy. The same asset argues for the Destination Upgrade: Yelp holds first-party behavioral data (a user's actual review history, bookings, and stated preferences) that a general-purpose answer engine structurally can't access, which is real personalization leverage if Yelp builds its own interface around it.

What tips the decision toward being genuinely hard, not just genuinely open, is that neither option is free, and 2026 EBITDA guidance already projects margin compression from the AI investment already committed. Yelp can't fully fund a world-class consumer AI experience, scale a data-licensing business, and rebuild its measurement and sales infrastructure at the same time. The case study explicitly frames this as a resource-prioritization decision, not a technology decision — the detail easiest to miss if you only look at the product options and skip the budget constraint sitting underneath them.

</Section>

<Section index="04" kicker="What to take away" title="The transferable lesson">

Any business whose model assumes customers arrive at a destination is exposed to the same fork the moment an AI layer starts answering questions on the destination's own content. Resisting disintermediation isn't the decision on the table. That ship has sailed. It sailed the same way for Attract generally. The real decision is choosing, deliberately, whether to compete for the interface (Destination) or to compete for being the trusted source behind everyone else's interface (Source Layer), and building the measurement (Influence Dashboard) that actually reflects whichever bet gets made, instead of continuing to report metrics built for the era that's ending.

What doesn't transfer: Yelp's specific leverage — a 330M+ review corpus with a real trust and content-integrity track record in an era when generative AI can flood the web with synthetic reviews — is not a generic asset every disintermediated business has. A company without a comparably hard-to-replicate content moat has a weaker Source Layer case, and the Destination Upgrade path gets correspondingly more attractive by default, not because it's easier.

</Section>
