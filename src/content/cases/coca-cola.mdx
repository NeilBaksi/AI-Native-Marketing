Turning brand rules into something a system enforces automatically, rather than a PDF a person has to remember, is what let Coca-Cola scale AI-generated content 10x without a compliance crisis, a pattern any brand or creative team maintaining guidelines across channels can apply. Coca-Cola runs 200+ brands across 200+ countries, each one filtered through local agencies and designers interpreting the same 400-page brand-guideline PDF. Before generative AI, that produced small, contained drift — a red that's slightly off, a logo sized wrong for a social crop. <Mark>Generative AI doesn't fix that drift; it multiplies it, instantly, at every market at once.</Mark> Four years before Coca-Cola shipped anything, its Global VP of Design asked the question that set the direction: not "how do we make more content faster," but *"what if our logo was intelligent enough to understand how it should behave within a design?"*

Instead of writing rules and handing them down, Fizzion watched senior designers work: this font at this size, this logo at this position. It captured those decisions as they happened, not a specification handed down for the system to enforce.

Every corrected decision became a "StyleID": a machine-readable file defining exactly how one brand element behaves. It specifies the red at a given opacity, the contour bottle at a given scale, the Sprite wordmark's spacing.

<Section index="03" kicker="What went right or wrong" title="A sound Design layer, a governance-zone miss">

<PullQuote quote="The Fizzion build shows what Design gets right when Brand DNA is genuinely infrastructure. Coca-Cola's own 2024 holiday ad shows what still goes wrong when that infrastructure covers the wrong risk." />

Fizzion is a governance success story for the category of failure it's built to catch: format drift, off-brand color, inconsistent logo placement. Each one is a rules-based check. StyleIDs enforce those well. It is a system built for content Design classifies as low-stakes and high-volume.

The 2024 holiday ad wasn't that kind of content. [Economics of Error](/spine/economics-of-error) already covers it in depth, worth reading in full there rather than repeated here. In short, Coca-Cola's AI remake of its 1995 holiday ad passed every brand-compliance rule Fizzion-style governance would check, and still landed emotionally flat. The gap wasn't in Fizzion's architecture. The holiday ad needed the human-only checkpoint of Zone 3 — content with brand-manifesto-level stakes — and got the fully-autonomous treatment of Zone 1 instead, because the rules-based checks all cleared. <Mark>A sound Design infrastructure still requires someone to correctly classify which zone a given piece of content belongs in before the infrastructure runs.</Mark>

</Section>

<Section index="04" kicker="What to take away" title="The transferable lesson">

Building excellent Design-layer infrastructure doesn't automatically solve governance. It solves the governance problems that infrastructure was built to catch. StyleIDs catch color, spacing, and placement drift because those are things a rules engine can verify. They cannot catch "does this feel true to the brand emotionally," because that's not a rule, it's a judgment call — precisely the [Economics of Error](/spine/economics-of-error) test for whether a decision belongs in a human-only zone. The transferable practice: before any content goes through an automated brand-governance system, classify its autonomy zone first, on its own stakes, not by whether the rules-based checks happen to be available for it.

What doesn't transfer: the object lesson here is Coca-Cola's own asset, at Coca-Cola's own scale of visibility. Most organizations won't have a single ad carry the reputational weight a Super Bowl-era holiday spot does for Coca-Cola specifically. The zone-classification discipline is universal. The blast radius when it's skipped is not.

</Section>
