<Section index="01" kicker="One layer, cascading" title="The five-layer messaging architecture">

Five layers exist, each inherited by the one below it. Change something at the top and every layer under it has to change too. Nothing downstream contradicts what's above it. Run it against [the toolkit's own worked example](/toolkit/positioning-statement) — Ridgeline, a fictional expense-and-procurement platform for mid-market finance teams — end to end:

Each layer is narrower and more specific than the one above it. By the time AI is generating channel-level copy at layer five, every word it produces can be traced back up the cascade to one sentence at the top.

<Section index="02" kicker="Two different jobs" title="Points of difference and points of parity">

Not every true claim belongs in the same place. A **point of difference (POD)** is a capability competitors can't credibly match, valued by the customer, and mapped to the exact JTBD dimension where the product wins. Ridgeline's editable routing logic is a POD, because most competitors keep that logic locked inside the vendor's own black box. A **point of parity (POP)** is table stakes: SOC 2 compliance, an audit trail, single sign-on. These are features every credible competitor also has. Ridgeline needs all of them to be considered at all, and none of them differentiates it from anything.

The failure mode this distinction exists to prevent: leading with a POP as if it were a POD. "We're SOC 2 compliant" as a headline claim invites a features checklist comparison Ridgeline wins by default and loses by framing, since every competitor clears that bar too, so leading with it says nothing except that the buyer should go compare the rest of the list. AI agents generating content from the messaging architecture must not promote a POP to differentiator status; the claims-and-proof layer is where that discipline gets enforced, not left to whichever pillar sounds most impressive in the moment.

</Section>

<Section index="03" kicker="Not optional" title="No stat without a source">

Every number that reaches layer three needs a registered, provenance-tagged source before AI can use it in generated content: verified, course-sourced, or explicitly synthetic, never unmarked. This isn't a style guideline for this handbook. It's mechanically enforced. Every `<Stat>` and `<Example>` on this site resolves against [a typed fact registry](/reference/statistics), and the site's own build fails if one doesn't. That includes this page's own tags — the same discipline it's teaching, applied to itself.

Skip this discipline in a real deployment and the failure mode isn't abstract: an AI system generating content at scale will confidently produce a claim that sounds exactly as authoritative as one that's actually sourced, because nothing in its output format distinguishes the two. The proof requirement is what makes that distinction visible before a claim ships, not after a customer catches it.
