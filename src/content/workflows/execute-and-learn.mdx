A decision ships. Execute and Learn are where it either proves itself or doesn't. The results become next cycle's starting priors, or, as usually happens, a PDF nobody opens again.

<Section index="01" kicker="Three fracture points" title="Always-on execution vs. campaign-based">

The conventional cycle runs plan, create, launch, monitor, review, and it breaks in three specific places, not vaguely: budgets lock quarterly and can't answer a real-time signal; optimization happens too late, after the money is already spent; and the loop stays open, because results land in a review deck that never touches the next campaign's starting assumptions.

<Section index="02" kicker="Proof, not description" title="Incrementality testing">

<Formula name="Incrementality" expression="Revenue with marketing − Revenue without marketing" />

Picture the audit version of this that doesn't hold up: every metric in the quarterly deck is up, and nobody in the room can produce a control group for a single one of them. That's not a hypothetical — it's the exact gap a holdout closes, and the reason the number below counts as proof only once one exists.

The only way to get the "without" side is a control group: a matched set of customers, same segment, same history, same lifecycle state, who simply didn't receive the campaign. <Mark>The gap between treatment and control is the only number in this whole handbook that actually proves causation rather than describing a correlation.</Mark>

Most dashboards skip the control group entirely. Spend and revenue both rise across a quarter, alongside seasonal demand nobody isolated, and the rise gets reported as the campaign's result. That's descriptive, not causal, and the difference matters most exactly when someone's about to defend a budget with the number.

Course material names this cluster the Measurement Gap: descriptive without causal (the failure above), attribution assigns credit rather than cause (the iROAS convention below exists to correct it), and the open loop (Section 01's third fracture point, results that never touch the next cycle's assumptions). A fourth trap sits alongside those three: what reads as a false positive changes by objective, since a lift that clears significance on click-through can vanish on revenue at the same sample size — which objective a test was actually powered for has to be checked before trusting either read.

</Section>

<Section index="03" kicker="The exact convention" title="iROAS">

<Formula name="iROAS" expression="Incremental Revenue ÷ Marketing Spend" />

Stated plainly once, here, because later toolkit pages restate this convention rather than redefine it: iROAS divides by spend, and the numerator is *incremental* revenue, the treatment-minus-control gap, not gross attributed revenue. A channel earns its keep. It clears this bar every cycle. If iROAS falls below 1, the spend cost more than it produced, full stop, regardless of what a last-touch attribution report claims elsewhere.

Platform-reported win rates are exactly the kind of number this bar is built to see through. A programmatic exchange can log a full bid-and-win cycle for an impression that never rendered on a real page — inflated delivery and win-rate numbers with no matching lift in a holdout group. iROAS doesn't care what a dashboard reports winning; it only cares what the control group didn't get that the treatment group did. The Trade Desk saw the same distortion across $10B+ of managed spend: clients running 12+ channels frequently watched attributed ROAS top 100%, multiple channels each claiming credit for one conversion, until a switch to incrementality-based buying corrected it.

<Section index="04" kicker="The finance-native version" title="ROMI">

<Formula name="ROMI" expression="(Incremental Contribution Margin − Marketing Spend) ÷ Marketing Spend" />

Same underlying proof as iROAS, restated in the currency finance actually tracks: margin, not revenue. iROAS answers "did this spend produce incremental revenue"; ROMI answers "did this spend produce incremental profit," which is the question a CFO is actually asking. Report iROAS to a growth-stage marketing team optimizing for revenue; report ROMI to a board deciding whether a channel is worth its cost once margin, not just top line, is on the table. The two numbers can disagree. A channel can clear an iROAS bar on low-margin products and fail a ROMI bar on the same spend.
