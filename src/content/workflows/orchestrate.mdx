Attract hands Orchestrate a customer in a known state. This chapter is deliberately sequenced here, before Build's more technical agent-construction chapter, so a concrete journey plays out on the page before the machinery that runs it does.

<Section index="01" kicker="Not a funnel" title="State-based customer journeys">

A funnel tells you where a customer has been. A state tells the system where they are right now — and a real state has to clear three tests: **observable** (the system can detect entry and exit on its own; "visited the pricing page three times this week" qualifies, "seems interested" doesn't), **exclusive** (one state at a time — being in two simultaneously produces conflicting instructions), and **actionable** (every state comes with a clear next move; a label with no move attached is just a label, and labels don't make money).

<Section index="02" kicker="Right message, channel, timing, person" title="Next-best-action selection">

Next-best-action is a real-time call for one customer, the moment a signal arrives, across four dimensions at once: the **right message** for where they are right now, not whatever campaign happens to be live; the **right channel** they're actually likely to engage with; the **right timing** — soon enough to matter, spaced enough not to wear them out; and the **right person** — someone who actually qualifies, isn't mid-sales-conversation, isn't on a suppression list.

Signal triggers fall into three types, and the useful ones read intent, not just activity: **intent signals** (pricing-page visits, comparison downloads), **behavioral signals** (an abandoned cart, a specific link clicked), and **lifecycle milestones** (day 30 usage check, day 60 renewal conversation). The same missed-day signal means something different for a casual learner than for someone with an exam in a week — reading which one it is, rather than firing the identical alert either way, is the difference between a system that responds to intent and one that just responds to activity.

</Section>

<Section index="03" kicker="Four checks, all must pass" title="The policy stack">

NBA doesn't fire on its own recommendation — it clears a stack of checks first, hard-wired into the decision rather than reviewed after it:

These aren't safety rails bolted on after the fact — nothing fires until all four pass, and only then does the system rank what's left by expected value (lift × business priority × recency). A do-not-contact list, channel-saturation limits, and an automatic sales-cycle exclusion in B2B (marketing goes quiet the moment a prospect enters an active deal, no exceptions) all sit inside this same stack, not bolted on beside it.

<Example id="jpmorgan-policy-stack">
  Credit card, mortgage, and wealth used to run independent outreach with no referee — a customer could receive three competing offers in 72 hours, and pre-approved mortgage offers sometimes reached people in active default conversations. One system now arbitrates across every product line: one message per customer per contact window, every candidate clearing fit, eligibility, compliance, and a frequency cap before it ships. Contact frequency dropped more than 30%, and the worst misfires simply stopped happening — not because anyone reviewed harder, but because the check now sits inside the decision instead of after it.
</Example>

## Where this leads

Orchestrate decides what should happen.
