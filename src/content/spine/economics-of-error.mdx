Economics of Error is a calculation, not a philosophy. A brand-strategy call and a bid-optimization tweak both sit inside the same zone of required human judgment, yet nobody treats them the same way. What decides the difference is a formula built on two variables, not instinct.

<Section index="01" kicker="The formula" title="Cost of error x value of speed">

The right level of AI autonomy is a function of how costly, visible, and reversible an error would be, weighed against how much is gained by deciding faster. Not "should AI be trusted here," which is a values question with no stable answer. A risk calculation, with two variables: <Mark>Cost of Error</Mark> (what's lost if the agent gets it wrong) and <Mark>Value of Speed</Mark> (what's gained from a faster autonomous decision).

<Section index="03" kicker="Applied" title="Reading the tiers against a real decision">

The formula is only useful if it's specific enough to catch a mistake before it ships, not just after. Design's Governance Zones are the economics of error applied to one workflow's content: roughly 50% of content volume sits in Zone 1 (full autonomy: product descriptions, routine transactional email), 30% in Zone 2 (human in the loop: health claims, financial offers, culturally sensitive messaging), and 20% in Zone 3 (human-only: crisis communications, brand manifestos). The split isn't arbitrary; it's the same cost/speed calculation, applied content type by content type.

<Example id="coca-cola-holiday-ad">
  Every brand-compliance rule passed. What was missing was a checkpoint for emotional resonance on content that should never have been Zone 1 in the first place. A holiday ad carries brand-equity stakes closer to a manifesto than a product description. This is autonomy inflation by another name: a Human-in-the-Loop decision (does this feel true to the brand?) got treated as though it were Fully Autonomous, simply because the rules-based checks all happened to pass.
</Example>

That's one symmetrical error. The other runs the opposite direction, <Mark>autonomy deflation</Mark>, requiring human sign-off on genuinely low-stakes, rapidly-correctable decisions and destroying the velocity AI exists to provide without buying any real governance benefit.

<Example id="netflix-ab-tests">
  Netflix runs over a thousand A/B tests at once, and a result never waits for a person to read a report. It updates the recommendation model directly. Routing that through human approval wouldn't make it safer; the cost of a wrong send-time or a losing test variant is low and self-correcting. Gating it anyway would be autonomy deflation at scale.
</Example>

The earlier chapter's Honda case is the canonical autonomy-inflation failure for the same reason Coca-Cola's ad is: a decision that needed a checkpoint (verifying consent) had that checkpoint quietly removed because removing it was efficient, until it wasn't.

</Section>

<Section index="04" kicker="Portable version" title="Why this becomes the recurring decision tool">

Every later workflow chapter runs the same calculation, and it compresses to four questions worth carrying forward as a unit:

<DefinitionList items={[
  { term: 'What is the cost of a wrong decision?', body: "An ICP error means the entire downstream system targets the wrong audience. A frequency-capping error means one person gets one extra email. Not the same decision." },
  { term: 'Is it reversible?', body: 'Rebuilding audience packages and re-aligning execution after a bad ICP call is expensive and slow to undo. Adjusting a bid in real time is not.' },
  { term: 'What is the value of acting faster?', body: 'Speed matters at the point of applying a criterion, not usually at the point of defining one. Getting the criterion right once beats getting it fast.' },
  { term: 'How confident is the AI?', body: 'Pattern detection produces candidates, not answers. Strategic fit, brand ambition, and competitive positioning are areas where the model has no informed opinion to be confident about.' },
]} />

Run those four questions against any decision, and the tier falls out on its own.
