<Section index="01" kicker="Falsification, filled in first" title="What it's for">

[Execute & Learn](/workflows/execute-and-learn) draws the line between a descriptive number and a causal one. A holdout exists precisely because spend and revenue both rising together proves nothing about which one caused the other. This worksheet operationalizes that discipline as a forcing function: <Mark>before a causal claim gets written down, state what would prove it wrong.</Mark> That's not a caveat added afterward. It's the first field on the page, filled in before the claim itself.

</Section>

<Section index="02" kicker="Before a causal claim ships" title="When to use it">

Fill this out before any sentence that says a channel or campaign "drove," "caused," or "generated" a result ships to a stakeholder. That's true whether or not a holdout actually exists behind the number.

- Before presenting an attribution, ROMI, or lift figure that a budget decision will lean on.
- Any time a result "obviously" supports the story someone already wanted to tell. That's precisely when a stated falsification test matters most.

</Section>

<Section index="06" kicker="Why falsification comes first" title="Design decision">

The field order is the whole design decision. "What would falsify this claim" sits before "what it would take to prove it" and before the confidence rating. That's deliberate. It gets written while the claim is still being formed, not bolted on as a caveat paragraph after the recommendation is already drafted and psychologically final. A humility check filled in last is easy to write around. <Mark>A falsification test that has to exist on paper before the claim does is much harder to quietly skip.</Mark>

Once filled in, the confidence rating travels with the claim into the deck or budget case it's headed for — the number a stakeholder reads next to the recommendation, not filed away separately from it.

</Section>
