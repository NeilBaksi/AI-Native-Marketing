This template forces a positioning claim into seven slots specific enough for a machine to reuse without a person interpreting it first. A paragraph of adjectives gives an AI system nothing to check itself against, and a wrong positioning claim follows into every downstream asset it assembles next — a landing page, an ad, a sales one-pager.

<Section index="01" kicker="A claim, not a slogan" title="What it's for">

[Positioning](/workflows/focus) already established that a positioning statement isn't a fourth strategic decision. It falls out of the ICP, the persona's JTBD, and where the brand draws its boundaries. This template is the fill-in artifact that turns that logic into seven testable slots: For, Who, [Product] is, That, Because, Unlike, and Our difference is. <Mark>Fill all seven honestly and there's nothing left to argue about in the next planning meeting.</Mark> The statement either holds up against a real competitor or it doesn't.

The test for a finished statement is the same one Design applies to brand guidelines. Can an agent act on this without a human translating it first? "We help businesses grow" fails. "For finance ops leaders losing days to manual approval routing" doesn't.

</Section>

<Section index="02" kicker="Before the messaging layer" title="When to use it">

Three moments call for this template: a new product or category launch, a repositioning after the market shifts under a product. It's also right whenever a team stands up an AI content pipeline that needs a single source-of-truth statement to load as its first prompt layer. <Mark>It's meant to be written once. It's revisited rarely, and referenced constantly.</Mark> Every messaging pillar, ad, and landing page downstream reads from it rather than re-deriving positioning per asset.

</Section>

<Section index="03" kicker="Filled in, for a fictional mid-market SaaS company" title="Worked example">

Ridgeline is a fictional expense-and-procurement automation platform sold to mid-market finance teams. It's used here to show the template filled in end to end, not as a real product.

<StepList
  steps={[
    { title: 'For', body: 'Finance operations leaders at mid-market companies (200–2,000 employees)' },
    { title: 'Who', body: 'Lose days each month reconciling expense approvals across five disconnected tools' },
    { title: 'Ridgeline is', body: 'An expense-and-procurement automation platform' },
    { title: 'That', body: 'Turns approval routing into a same-day, audit-ready process' },
    { title: 'Because', body: "It reads existing ERP and card-feed data directly — no manual export" },
    { title: 'Unlike', body: 'Legacy expense tools that digitize the paper form but not the approval logic' },
    { title: 'Our difference is', body: "The routing rules are visible and editable by finance, not locked in a vendor's black box" },
  ]}
/>

Read end to end, it argues its own case: a specific buyer, a specific cost, a specific mechanism, a specific reason the alternative falls short.

</Section>

<Section index="06" kicker="Why seven slots, not a paragraph" title="Design decision">

<Callout kind="key" title="Constraint beats latitude">
An unconstrained positioning brief tends toward a paragraph of adjectives — "innovative," "customer-first," "best-in-class" — because an open format has no mechanism that forces a specific claim to the surface. Seven named slots remove that latitude structurally: each one demands a fact, not a feeling, and a statement with a blank slot reads as visibly unfinished rather than vaguely unconvincing. That's something an AI system can load as a constraint, not a mood board.
</Callout>
