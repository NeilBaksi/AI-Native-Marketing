<Section index="01" kicker="One convention, restated" title="What it's for">

[Execute & Learn](/workflows/execute-and-learn) defines iROAS and ROMI and states the site's exact convention once: iROAS divides *incremental* revenue — the treatment-minus-control gap from a holdout test, not gross attributed revenue — by spend. This page restates that convention plainly, once, as a reference, and then adds what that chapter doesn't: a worksheet for plugging in your own numbers.

<Formula
  name="ROMI"
  expression="(Incremental Contribution Margin − Marketing Spend) ÷ Marketing Spend"
  terms={[
    { symbol: 'iROAS', meaning: 'Incremental Revenue ÷ Marketing Spend. Incremental means the holdout-tested gap, not everything a last-touch report attributes to the channel.' },
    { symbol: 'Contribution Margin', meaning: 'Revenue × (1 − COGS% − Returns Rate) — what is actually left after cost of goods and returns, before marketing spend is subtracted.' },
    { symbol: 'Gross Profit', meaning: 'Revenue × (1 − COGS%) — Contribution Margin minus the returns adjustment.' },
    { symbol: 'Returns Cost', meaning: 'Revenue × Returns Rate — the share of incremental revenue that comes back as a return and never becomes real margin.' },
  ]}
  note="Same proof as iROAS, restated in the currency finance actually tracks — profit, not revenue."
/>

</Section>

<Section index="02" kicker="Before a number reaches a deck" title="When to use it">

Reach for this worksheet whenever a channel or campaign result is about to become a number in a deck — a quarterly review, a budget-reallocation case, or a check against a ROMI figure already cited elsewhere on this site.

- Reporting channel performance where iROAS and ROMI disagree, and someone needs to see why.
- Building the financial case for shifting spend, where the answer has to survive a CFO asking what margin assumption it used.

</Section>

<Section index="03" kicker="Worked at three COGS levels" title="Worked example">

A channel ran a holdout test: $50,000 spend, $180,000 in incremental revenue attributable to the treatment group over the control. COGS is assumed at 30%, returns at 5%.

<ComparisonTable
  caption="ROMI worked at the assumed COGS, then re-run at ±5 points to check how much the conclusion depends on that assumption."
  columns={['Contribution Margin', 'ROMI']}
  rows={[
    { label: 'COGS 25% (low end)', cells: ['$180,000 × 0.70 = $126,000', '($126,000 − $50,000) ÷ $50,000 = 152%'] },
    { label: 'COGS 30% (assumed)', cells: ['$180,000 × 0.65 = $117,000', '($117,000 − $50,000) ÷ $50,000 = 134%'] },
    { label: 'COGS 35% (high end)', cells: ['$180,000 × 0.60 = $108,000', '($108,000 − $50,000) ÷ $50,000 = 116%'] },
  ]}
/>

iROAS on the same spend is a simpler line — $180,000 ÷ $50,000 = 3.6x — and <Mark>doesn't move with the COGS assumption at all, which is exactly why ROMI, not iROAS, is the number that needs the sensitivity check</Mark>. Here the channel clears a positive ROMI at both ends of a reasonable COGS range, so the decision doesn't hinge on getting the assumption exactly right.

<Section index="06" kicker="Why ROAS isn't in the set" title="Design decision">

ROAS — gross revenue over spend, with no incrementality or margin in it — deliberately isn't in the reference set above. It's reference-only where it appears elsewhere, never the number a decision leans on, because <Mark>it can't fail: a channel with terrible margins and zero incremental effect can still post a large ROAS.</Mark> The other non-obvious habit worth carrying forward: state the COGS assumption out loud and re-run the number at a band around it, every time. A ROMI figure is only as trustworthy as the margin assumption behind it, and the sensitivity check is what turns "trust this number" into something a reader can actually audit. That audit trail is what a number needs before it survives a quarterly review or a budget-reallocation case — the sensitivity table travels with the headline figure into that deck, not filed away once the deck ships.

</Section>
