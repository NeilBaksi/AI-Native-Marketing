<Section index="01" kicker="The setup" title="Portfolio complexity as an internal marketing problem">

Salesforce is shifting Agentforce from per-seat subscription pricing to consumption-based pricing, paying per conversation, per action, per outcome, across four product lines that different stakeholders in the same account buy for different reasons.

<Stat id="salesforce-scenario-setup" />

Six student teams worked the same capstone dataset toward different commercial objectives. Five took an external angle. That meant new logos, expansion, competitive response. Team 6 got the outlier assignment: Internal Marketing / Revenue Model Transition. Reps are losing deals they should win because they can't make the new pricing make sense out loud, in front of a customer. Team 6 named that symptom consumption confusion.

<Example id="capstone-engineered-trap">
The dataset buries a deliberate trap in the enablement table: a cluster of reps with well over 80% training completion, but a confidence score of 1 or 2 out of 5 and a deal-attach rate under 10%. Training completion, on its own, turns out to be a vanity metric. <Mark>A rep can sit through every module and still be unable to defend the bill.</Mark>
</Example>

Read that way, portfolio complexity isn't only a sales-enablement gap to patch with another course. It's also a problem the company is failing to market to itself, though a messaging fix addresses discoverability, not the underlying pricing complexity reps are struggling to explain.

</Section>

<Section index="02" kicker="The reframe" title="Treat the rep as the customer">

Team 6's central move: run the same discipline on reps that the rest of this handbook runs on external buyers. The ICP is internal. The signals are enablement data, training completion, confidence scores, deal-attach rates, loss reasons, billing escalations. The deal is an Agentforce attach, not a purchase.

<Stat id="capstone-inverse-paradox" />

The ICP work runs a four-signal-family triangulation, cognitive readiness, behavioral breakdowns, operational friction, commercial outcome, and each family is defined as much by what it excludes as by what it keeps. Quota attainment, for instance, gets dropped entirely: it shows no correlation with the underlying problem, because reps experiencing it were masking the shortfall with other deals. The 200-rep population narrows through a three-level filter: role, then behavioral signal, then trigger event, funneling down to the cohort the program actually targets.

<Mark>Naming who the program is *not* for turns out to matter as much as naming who it is for.</Mark> Most enablement plans skip that negative persona entirely.

</Section>

<Section index="03" kicker="The formula" title="The iROAS convention, restated">

The iROAS this page uses is [incremental return divided by incremental cost](/workflows/execute-and-learn) — not (return minus cost) divided by cost. Both conventions exist in practice; every number below states which one it is, because silently teaching one as *the* definition is a live mis-teaching risk.

<Callout kind="caution" title="A shipping threshold, not a forecast">
The deck itself calls its own +5-percentage-point attach lift and 4.9× iROAS "a shipping threshold, not a forecast." None of these outcomes exist in the dataset yet. Every figure below is an a-priori target the pilot will be held to, not a result it already produced.
</Callout>

The chain: a 35-rep Tier-1 cohort carries $11.3M in ACV (annual contract value) exposure. A baseline Agentforce attach rate of 4.5%, corrected mid-project from an overstated 8%, targets a lift to 9.5%, five points higher. Five points against $11.3M is $565K of incremental Agentforce revenue; applying a 1.3× per-deal expansion multiplier (flagged by the team itself as an assumption to validate) brings incremental return to $735K against a $150K program cost.

<Section index="04" kicker="The build" title="What the capstone built">

The program itself is a four-state journey with boolean entry and exit conditions — a rep doesn't advance on a manager's judgment call, but on measured thresholds crossed and sustained.

<ComparisonTable
  caption="The four-state journey, entry gate and autonomy per state"
  columns={['Entry (boolean)', 'Autonomy']}
  rows={[
    { label: '01 Trained-but-Stuck', cells: ['Training ≥80% AND confidence ≤2 AND attach <10%, sustained 30 days', 'L4, fully autonomous enrollment'] },

    { label: '03 Confident Operator', cells: ['Confidence ≥4 AND attach ≥25% AND override <30%', 'L2, recommends, manager approves'] },

  ]}
/>

A regression rule reverts a rep one state if confidence drops or billing escalations spike, with a 14-day cooldown before the next check, specifically to stop the state machine from flapping a rep back and forth on noise. Budget follows the same logic: a $150K pilot envelope, split roughly 70/30 internal to external, moves between states on five reallocation triggers rather than a fixed calendar. One of those triggers is a hard kill-switch. A 20% rise in billing escalations inside 30 days pauses the AI layer entirely and routes the rep to a human enablement lead.

<Callout kind="note" title="The agent layer underneath">
The deck's own six-agent system, INTAKE, STATE, PULSE, ROUTER, WARDEN, LEDGER, is what actually runs this journey and its budget logic day to day. WARDEN's kill-switch, the budget's reallocation trigger above, and the program's governance table all name the same mechanism three times, in three different sections of the deck. The [economics of error](/spine/economics-of-error) formula behind it is identical to the one this handbook uses everywhere else, just labeled by level (L1–L4) instead of by name. A fully worked six-agent design, built for a different problem, is in the [Agentic Lab](/applied/agentic-lab).
</Callout>
